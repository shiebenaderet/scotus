Kennedy v. Bremerton (2022)

Background Information

Understanding the facts and constitutional questions

Connecting to Our Guiding Question

When, if ever, should the government be allowed to limit a person's constitutional rights in order to protect the community?

This case asks whether a public school may limit a coach's First Amendment right to practice his religion by restricting where he prays. The district said it acted to protect students from religious coercion. It also wanted to avoid looking like it endorsed a religion. As you read, weigh the coach's religious freedom against the community's interest in keeping government neutral about religion.

This case asks: May a public school stop a coach from kneeling to pray at midfield after games? The district worried students would feel pressure to join, or that the school was endorsing a religion. The coach says a short personal prayer is Free Exercise. When may a school limit an employee’s religious expression at a school event?

This case asks: Can a public school stop a coach from praying on the field after games? The school was worried. Players might feel they had to join. It might also look like the school was choosing a religion. The coach said his short prayer was his own. So when can a school limit how a worker prays at a school event?

What Happened?

Joseph Kennedy was a football coach at Bremerton High School in Washington state. He began coaching there in 2008, and from his first season he made a practice of praying on the field after games. He has said a movie about a praying coach, Facing the Giants, inspired him.

After each game, Kennedy would walk to the 50-yard line of the football field, kneel down, and offer a brief, quiet prayer of thanks. Initially, Kennedy prayed alone. However, some players noticed what he was doing and asked if they could join him. Kennedy said he told them, "This is a free country. You can do what you want." He has said he never required or asked players to pray with him.

Over time, the post-game prayers grew. After some games most of the team joined, and sometimes players from the other team. Kennedy also began giving short postgame talks at midfield that included religious references, and he led locker-room prayers, a team tradition that started before he arrived. For over seven years, no one complained to the district.

In September 2015, an employee from another school district attended a game and noticed the prayer. This person praised the prayers to Bremerton's principal. That is how the district learned about them.

The school district became worried about potential legal problems. They were concerned that having a coach pray publicly might violate the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment, which prohibits government endorsement of religion.

On September 17, 2015, the district told Kennedy to stop the religious talks and the prayers with students. He stopped them. But he told the district he would keep saying a short prayer alone at midfield after games, and he offered to pray while players were walking to the locker room or bus. The district said he could pray only in a private place that students and the public could not see. Kennedy refused.

On October 16, 2015, after Kennedy had told reporters about his plan, he prayed on the field again after the homecoming game. Players from the other team and members of the community joined him. Television cameras surrounded the group, and people jumped fences to reach the field, knocking over student band members.

After he prayed again following the October 23 and 26 games, the district placed Kennedy on paid administrative leave. His evaluation recommended that he not be rehired, and he did not return the next season. Kennedy sued, claiming the school violated his First Amendment rights.

The Big Question

Can a public school coach engage in personal prayer on school property where students and the public can see him? Or does this violate the separation of church and state?

What You Need to Know

The First Amendment has two clauses about religion:

Free Exercise Clause: "Congress shall make no law...prohibiting the free exercise" of religion. This protects people's right to practice their faith.

Establishment Clause: "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion." This prevents the government from endorsing or favoring any religion.

These two clauses sometimes seem to conflict - protecting religious freedom while preventing government endorsement of religion.

Important Facts

  • Kennedy's prayers were brief (about 30 seconds) and quiet
  • He initially prayed alone, then others voluntarily joined
  • Kennedy said he never required or asked students to participate, but some players and parents said students felt they had to join to stay part of the team
  • The prayers continued for years without complaints
  • The school offered a private place to pray. Kennedy offered to pray while players headed to the locker room or bus, but refused to stop praying on the field
  • At the October 16 game, people rushed the field to join the prayer and knocked over student band members

Vocabulary

Establishment Clause: Constitutional provision preventing government endorsement of religion
Free Exercise Clause: Constitutional provision protecting the right to practice religion
Separation of Church and State: The principle that government and religion should remain separate
Administrative Leave: Temporary suspension from job duties while an investigation occurs

What Happened?

Joseph Kennedy coached football at Bremerton High School in Washington State. He started in 2008. From his first season, he knelt at the 50-yard line after each game for a short, quiet prayer of about 30 seconds. He has said a movie about a praying coach, Facing the Giants, inspired him.

At first he prayed alone. Over time, players joined him, and sometimes players from the other team did too. Kennedy also gave short talks after games that included religious messages, and he led prayers in the locker room. For more than seven years, no one complained to the district.

In September 2015, an employee from another school district saw the prayer and praised it to Bremerton’s principal. The district worried that a coach praying with students could break the Establishment Clause. On September 17, 2015, it sent Kennedy a letter telling him to stop the religious talks and the prayers with students. He stopped those.

But Kennedy said he would keep praying alone at midfield. He offered to pray while players walked to the locker room or bus. The district offered him a private place to pray that students and the public could not see. He refused. After he told reporters his plan, he prayed on the field after the October 16, 2015 homecoming game. People jumped fences onto the field and knocked over members of the student band.

He prayed on the field after two more games. The district then put him on paid leave, and he did not return the next season. He sued. He said the district broke his Free Exercise and free-speech rights. The district answered with the Establishment Clause.

The question

May a public school stop a coach from praying at midfield after games because it looks like the school is endorsing religion?

Important facts

  • Two First Amendment clauses pull against each other here: Free Exercise and Establishment.
  • Kennedy said no one had to join. Some players and parents said students felt they had to join to stay part of the team.
  • A key question is who is speaking: the man himself, or the school through its employee.
  • In Santa Fe v. Doe (2000), the Supreme Court struck down school-sponsored prayer at football games. Both sides will use or distinguish it.

Vocabulary

Free Exercise Clause: Bars the government from stopping people from practicing their religion.
Establishment Clause: Bars the government from setting up or favoring a religion, or pressuring people to join one.
Government speech: When the speaker is the school, or its employee on duty, the Establishment Clause applies.

The question

Can a public school stop a coach from praying on the field where students and others can see him?

What you need

The First Amendment has two parts about religion. One part protects your right to practice your religion. It is called the Free Exercise Clause. The other part says the government may not set up or favor a religion. It is called the Establishment Clause. In this case, the two parts pull in different directions.

  • The prayers were short and quiet, about 30 seconds.
  • He prayed alone at first. Then others joined.
  • Kennedy said no one had to join. Some players and parents said students felt they had to.
  • The school offered a private place to pray. Kennedy wanted to keep praying on the field.
  • On October 16, people rushed onto the field and knocked over band members.

Vocabulary

Religion: Beliefs about God, gods, or what is sacred, and how people practice them.
Free Exercise Clause: The part of the First Amendment that protects your right to practice your religion.
Establishment Clause: The part that says the government may not set up or favor a religion.

Argument Answer
Praying at midfield was an important part of his personal religious practice. Evidence: Kennedy has said that before his first game in 2008, after watching the movie Facing the Giants, he promised God he would give thanks after every game. He kept that practice for 7 years. His prayers lasted about 30 seconds.
Accommodating his prayer practice does not violate the Establishment Clause. Evidence: The First Amendment has two religion clauses. The Establishment Clause says government may not establish a religion; the Free Exercise Clause says government may not prohibit religious practice. Kennedy argues a short personal prayer is Free Exercise, not a school-led worship service.
A reasonable observer would think the school was endorsing Christianity when they saw the coach praying. Evidence: Kennedy prayed at the 50-yard line — the most visible spot on the field — while wearing his Bremerton coaching gear. Photos show him surrounded by players in uniform. A spectator would reasonably think this was a school-sponsored religious activity.
Students might feel pressured to join prayer to stay on the team or please their coach. Evidence: Coaches decide who gets playing time. Principal Polm testified that a parent said a son felt he had to join the prayers to get playing time. Kennedy said he never required anyone to participate. The district argues the coach’s power still creates pressure.
The Free Exercise Clause protects his right to pray according to his faith. Evidence: The First Amendment's "Free Exercise Clause" says the government cannot prohibit people from practicing their religion. The Supreme Court has ruled this protection applies to all Americans — including government employees like teachers and coaches.
After Kennedy publicized his plan to pray, crowds and media rushed the field. Evidence: On October 16, 2015, after Kennedy announced he would pray publicly, media and spectators rushed onto the field. People jumped fences to reach the field and knocked over student band members. Bremerton players were singing the fight song; people from the crowd and the opposing team reached Kennedy at midfield.
The government cannot force religious people to hide their faith or pray only in private. Evidence: In Santa Fe Independent School District v. Doe (2000), the Court treated prayer at a football game as school-sponsored even when it was labeled student-led. Kennedy argues his kneeling is the opposite: a personal prayer, not a loudspeaker program. The district argues midfield in coaching gear still looks like a school ritual.
The school offered reasonable alternatives - Kennedy could pray in a private place. Evidence: The school district offered Kennedy multiple alternatives: a private place in the school building, athletic facility, or press box, or praying on the field after the stadium emptied. Kennedy could still practice his faith — just not in a way that looked like school endorsement.
Schools have a special responsibility to avoid even the appearance of religious endorsement. Evidence: In Lee v. Weisman (1992), the Court banned school-sponsored graduation prayers. In Santa Fe v. Doe (2000), the Court banned student-led prayers at football games. Schools must be especially careful because students are young and impressionable.
His prayers were personal, brief, and quiet - not attempts to convert students or promote religion. Evidence: Kennedy's prayers lasted about 30 seconds, and he knelt with his head bowed. Kennedy said he never asked students to join. He had given postgame talks with religious references, but he stopped them after the district's September 2015 letter.
The Establishment Clause requires schools to remain neutral on religious matters. Evidence: The Establishment Clause (part of the First Amendment) says government "shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion." This means public schools — which are part of the government — cannot favor any religion or appear to promote religious practices.
When a government employee prays publicly, it appears that the government endorses that religion. Evidence: Kennedy was a public school employee being paid with taxpayer money to coach students. When he prayed on the school's field, in school gear, surrounded by students, it looked like the school (a government institution) was endorsing Christianity.
Public employees retain their First Amendment rights even while working. Evidence: In Pickering v. Board of Education (1968), the Supreme Court said teachers cannot be forced to "relinquish the First Amendment rights they would otherwise enjoy as citizens." Teachers can wear religious jewelry, coaches can attend church — working for the government doesn't mean giving up all religious expression.
He never required, asked, or pressured students to join his prayers. Evidence: Kennedy testified he never asked any student to pray with him. Students joined on their own. In Lee v. Weisman (1992), the Supreme Court wrote that facing speech you disagree with "is part of learning how to live in a pluralistic society."

Key Terms for Arguments

Important concepts to understand when debating this case

Coercion

Pressure or force that makes someone feel they must participate in religious activity.

Government Endorsement

When government actions appear to favor or promote religion.

Reasonable Observer

A legal test that asks whether a typical person would think the government is endorsing religion. The District relied on this test. The Court was asked whether to keep using it.

Coercion

Pressure that makes someone feel they have to join a religious activity. They may not want to, but they feel they must.

Government endorsement

When the government seems to back a religion. A public school is part of the government.

Connecting to Today

How these constitutional questions still matter

The same questions about religion in public schools have come up again and again. Here are three examples, each with a date.

1992

Prayer at Graduation

In Lee v. Weisman, decided June 24, 1992, the Supreme Court ruled on a public middle school in Providence, Rhode Island, whose principal had invited a rabbi to give prayers at graduation. The Court ruled that the school could not do this, because students could feel pressure to take part.

Source: Lee v. Weisman, 505 U.S. 577 (1992)

Think about: Is there a difference between a student leading prayer and a teacher doing so?

2024 to 2026

The Ten Commandments in Classrooms

A 2024 Louisiana law requires every public school classroom to display a poster of the Ten Commandments. A federal judge blocked the law in November 2024. On February 20, 2026, the full U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit lifted that order, saying it was too early to decide whether the law is constitutional. As of that date, the case was not over.

Sources: ACLU, February 20, 2026; Roake v. Brumley (5th Cir. 2026)

Think about: Is a poster the state requires different from a teacher choosing to share a personal belief?

Since 1984

Student Religious Clubs

Since August 11, 1984, a federal law called the Equal Access Act has said that a public high school that lets student clubs meet must not turn a club away because of the religious, political, or other content of what its members say.

Source: 20 U.S.C. § 4071

Think about: Should schools treat religious clubs the same as other clubs like chess or drama?

Discussion Questions

  • How can schools respect both religious freedom and the separation of church and state?
  • Does it matter if participation in religious activity is truly voluntary, or can students still feel pressured?
