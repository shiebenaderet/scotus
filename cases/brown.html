Brown v. Board of Education (1954)

Reading:

Background Information

Understanding the facts and constitutional questions

🔗 Connecting to Our Guiding Question

When, if ever, should the government be allowed to limit a person's constitutional rights in order to protect the community?

This case asks: Does the Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause allow a state to separate children by race in public schools if it claims the buildings are equal? This is not mainly a “safety vs. rights” case — it is about what “equal protection of the laws” requires.

This case asks: Is it constitutional for a state to make Black students and white students go to different public schools, even if the buildings look similar? The big idea is equal protection — treating people the same under the law — not “keeping the community safe.”

What Happened?

In the 1950s, 17 states had laws requiring racial segregation in public schools. Kansas law allowed, but did not require, some cities to separate elementary schools, and Topeka did. These laws said that Black children and white children could not attend the same schools. This system was called "separate but equal" - the idea that as long as both races had schools, it was constitutional to keep them separate.

Linda Brown was a Black third-grader who lived in Topeka, Kansas. Every day, Linda had to walk six blocks through a dangerous railroad switchyard, then take a bus to Monroe Elementary School, the school designated for Black children. This journey was long and potentially dangerous.

However, there was another elementary school, Sumner School, just seven blocks from Linda's home. Linda could not attend Sumner School because it was designated for white children only.

In 1950, the Topeka branch of the NAACP (National Association for the Advancement of Colored People), led by McKinley Burnett, set out to challenge the Kansas law. It recruited 13 parents, including Oliver Brown. Following their lawyers' advice, the parents tried to enroll their children in white schools. Sumner School refused to admit Linda because of her race.

In February 1951 the Topeka NAACP filed the case for all 13 families. The Supreme Court later heard it together with similar cases from South Carolina, Virginia, and Delaware, and decided a fifth case from Washington, D.C., on the same day. Thurgood Marshall, the NAACP's chief legal counsel, led the legal team that argued the case before the Supreme Court. Marshall would later become the first Black Supreme Court Justice in 1967.

The Big Question

Does racial segregation in public schools violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment? Even if the separate schools are equal in quality?

What You Need to Know

The Fourteenth Amendment says:

"No State shall...deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws."

This means all people must be treated equally under the law.

The previous legal doctrine of "separate but equal" came from an 1896 case called Plessy v. Ferguson, which said segregation was constitutional as long as the facilities were equal.

Important Facts

  • Linda Brown was in third grade when the case began
  • She had to travel much farther to school than necessary because of segregation
  • The Court heard five school cases together: four from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, and Delaware, and one from Washington, D.C.
  • Thurgood Marshall, the NAACP's lead attorney, argued the case before the Supreme Court
  • Even when facilities appeared equal, segregation caused psychological harm to Black children
  • The NAACP used social science research to show the harmful effects of segregation
  • "Separate but equal" had been the rule since Plessy v. Ferguson (1896), nearly 60 years earlier

Vocabulary

Segregation: The practice of separating people based on race
Equal Protection Clause: Part of the Fourteenth Amendment requiring equal treatment under law
Separate but Equal: The legal doctrine that segregation was acceptable if facilities were equal
NAACP: National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, a civil rights organization
Precedent: Previous court decisions that guide future cases

What Happened?

In the 1950s Kansas law allowed cities to run racially separate public elementary schools under “separate but equal.” The Topeka board used that law for grades 1–6. Junior and senior high were not segregated.

Linda Brown, a Black third grader, walked through a railroad yard and rode a bus to Monroe Elementary. Sumner Elementary, for white students, was about seven blocks from her home. The board would not enroll her there.

Oliver Brown and other parents, with NAACP lawyers, sued. They argued that assigning students by race violates the Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause even if buildings look similar. The board argued Plessy v. Ferguson still allowed separate-but-equal schools, and that Topeka’s facilities were substantially equal.

The question

May a state require racially separate public schools if it claims the buildings are equal?

Important facts

  • This is an equal-protection case, not a “safety vs. rights” case.
  • The trial court found segregation harmful but still followed Plessy because facilities looked equal.
  • Sweatt v. Painter (1950) had already said “equal” can mean more than bricks — reputation and opportunity count.

Vocabulary

Equal Protection Clause: The Fourteenth Amendment rule that no state may deny any person “the equal protection of the laws.”
Separate but equal: The Plessy idea that racial separation is legal if facilities are equal. The families say that idea is the problem.
Precedent: An earlier decision courts are asked to follow — here, Plessy — unless a later Court overrules it.

Brown Family

Segregated schools violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Separate schools are inherently unequal because segregation stamps Black children with a badge of inferiority.

Board of Education

The Constitution doesn't prohibit separate schools as long as facilities are equal. States have authority over education, and "separate but equal" has been accepted law since Plessy v. Ferguson.

The Two Sides

Brown Family

Race-based school assignment violates equal protection even if the buildings look similar. Separate is the inequality.

Board of Education

Plessy still controls. If facilities are equal, states may run schools as they always have. Education is a local matter the Constitution does not mention.

Argument Answer
Government cannot classify and separate people based on their race. Evidence: The 14th Amendment (ratified July 9, 1868) states: "nor deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws."
States should have the right to organize their school systems based on local traditions. Evidence: In 1954, 17 states plus D.C. required school segregation by law, while 16 states prohibited it — showing states made their own choices.
Separating people by race automatically suggests that one race is inferior to another. Evidence: In Drs. Kenneth and Mamie Clark's doll tests, most Black children preferred the white dolls, and many said the Black dolls were "bad."
Education is too important to allow racial discrimination in public schools. Evidence: In the 1949–50 school year, a survey of six Deep South states found that spending per Black student was only 65 percent of spending per white student, showing "equal" schools were often not equal.
The Fourteenth Amendment was not originally intended to require integrated schools. Evidence: The same Congress that proposed the 14th Amendment in 1866 also funded segregated schools in Washington D.C. — showing they didn't intend to ban segregation.
Courts have accepted "separate but equal" for nearly 60 years, so judges should follow that precedent. Evidence: Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) established "separate but equal" as the law for 58 years. In 1951, the federal trial court in Topeka still followed it.
Research shows that segregation causes psychological harm to Black children. Evidence: The Topeka trial court found that segregation "has a detrimental effect upon the colored children." For the South Carolina case, Kenneth Clark repeated his doll test with Black children in Clarendon County.
In Topeka, a court found the Black and white schools had similar buildings, teachers, and materials. Evidence: The U.S. District Court in Kansas found Topeka's Black and white schools had substantially equal curricula, qualified teachers, and similar facilities.
Even when facilities look equal, segregated schools are inferior in reputation and opportunities. Evidence: In Sweatt v. Painter (1950), the Supreme Court ruled that a new Black law school lacked the "reputation," "influence of the alumni," and "prestige" of the white school, and it also had fewer faculty, courses, and books.
The Fourteenth Amendment requires that all people be treated equally under the law. Evidence: The Equal Protection Clause had already been used to strike down discrimination. In 1954, 21 states had laws requiring or permitting school segregation.
Forcing integration could cause social problems and conflicts in communities. Evidence: In November 1952, South Carolina voters approved a change to the state constitution that let the legislature close public schools rather than integrate them.
The "separate but equal" rule has been the law since 1896, and courts have followed it ever since. Evidence: Plessy v. Ferguson was precedent for 58 years. Courts follow a rule called "stare decisis" (Latin for "stand by what was decided") — meaning they try to follow past rulings to keep the law consistent.

Key Terms for Arguments

Important concepts to understand when debating this case

Inherently Unequal

The idea that separation itself creates inequality, regardless of the quality of facilities.

Psychological Harm

The emotional and mental damage caused by segregation, particularly to children.

Social Science Evidence

Research studies showing the harmful effects of segregation on children.

Separate is Not Equal

Even if things look the same, keeping people apart makes some people feel worse than others.

Hurt Feelings

Forced racial separation can damage how children see themselves — even when the buildings look similar.

Connecting to Today

How these constitutional questions still matter

Questions about educational equality and segregation remain relevant more than 70 years after this case. Consider these modern situations:

School Funding

Resource Inequalities

Today, schools in wealthier neighborhoods often have more resources, better facilities, and more experienced teachers than schools in poorer areas. Many argue this creates a new form of unequal education.

Think about: Does "equal protection" require equal school funding, or just the same legal access?

Demographics

Neighborhood Segregation

Many schools remain largely separated by race - not because of laws, but because neighborhoods are segregated. This is called "de facto" (in practice) segregation versus "de jure" (by law) segregation.

Think about: Should the Equal Protection Clause address segregation that happens without explicit laws?

Current Debate

School Choice Programs

Some advocate for charter schools, vouchers, or magnet schools to give families more options. Others worry these programs could increase segregation by allowing families to leave diverse public schools.

Think about: How do we balance family choice with the goal of integrated education?

Discussion Questions

  • If schools are no longer segregated by law but remain mostly one race, is that still a constitutional problem?
  • How might unequal school funding relate to the Equal Protection Clause?
  • Should the Constitution guarantee equal educational opportunities, or just equal legal treatment?

Questions about making schools fair for everyone are still important today. Here are some examples:

In the News

Some Schools Have More

Schools in rich neighborhoods often have nicer buildings, newer computers, and more activities. Schools in poor neighborhoods sometimes don't have as much.

What do you think: Is this fair? What should we do about it?

Where Kids Live

Neighborhoods Stay Separate

Even though there's no law keeping kids apart, many schools have mostly one race because of where families live. Different neighborhoods have different schools.

What do you think: Should we try to mix schools up more?

Big Question

Choosing Your School

Some families want to pick which school their kids go to. But some people worry this might make schools less mixed.

What do you think: Should families be able to pick any school they want?

Questions to Think About

  • If schools aren't separated by rules anymore but kids of different races still go to different schools, is that still unfair?
  • Is it okay for some schools to have way more money than others?
  • Should the government make sure every kid gets the same quality of education?
