What Happened?

In the 1950s, 17 states had laws requiring racial segregation in public schools. Kansas law allowed, but did not require, some cities to separate elementary schools, and Topeka did. These laws said that Black children and white children could not attend the same schools. This system was called "separate but equal." Under it, keeping the races apart was treated as constitutional as long as both races had schools.

Linda Brown was a Black third-grader who lived in Topeka, Kansas. Every day, Linda walked six blocks through a dangerous railroad switchyard. Then she took a bus to Monroe Elementary School, the school set aside for Black children.

However, there was another elementary school, Sumner School, just seven blocks from Linda's home. Linda could not attend Sumner School because it was designated for white children only.

In 1950, the Topeka branch of the NAACP (National Association for the Advancement of Colored People), led by McKinley Burnett, set out to challenge the Kansas law. It recruited 13 parents, including Oliver Brown. Following their lawyers' advice, the parents tried to enroll their children in white schools. Sumner School refused to admit Linda because of her race.

In February 1951 the Topeka NAACP filed the case for all 13 families. The Supreme Court later heard it together with similar cases from South Carolina, Virginia, and Delaware. It decided a fifth case, from Washington, D.C., on the same day. Thurgood Marshall, the NAACP's chief legal counsel, led the legal team that argued the case before the Supreme Court. Marshall would later become the first Black Supreme Court Justice in 1967.

The Big Question

Does racial segregation in public schools violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment? Does the answer change if the separate schools are equal in quality?

What You Need to Know

The Fourteenth Amendment says:

"No State shall...deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws."

This means all people must be treated equally under the law.

The rule of "separate but equal" came from an 1896 case called Plessy v. Ferguson. That case said segregation was constitutional as long as the facilities were equal.

Important Facts

  • Linda Brown was in third grade when the case began
  • She had to travel much farther to school than necessary because of segregation
  • The Court heard five school cases together: four from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, and Delaware, and one from Washington, D.C.
  • Thurgood Marshall, the NAACP's lead attorney, argued the case before the Supreme Court
  • The Kansas trial court found that segregation harmed Black children, even though it also found Topeka's Black and white schools substantially equal
  • The NAACP used social science research to show the harmful effects of segregation
  • "Separate but equal" had been the rule since Plessy v. Ferguson (1896), nearly 60 years earlier

What Happened?

In the 1950s, Kansas law allowed some cities to run separate public elementary schools for Black and white children. This was called “separate but equal.” Topeka used that law for grades 1 to 6. Its junior and senior high schools were not separated by race.

Linda Brown was a Black third grader in Topeka. She walked through a railroad yard and rode a bus to Monroe Elementary, a school for Black children. Sumner Elementary, a school for white children, was about seven blocks from her home.

In 1950, the Topeka branch of the NAACP decided to challenge the law. It asked 13 parents to help, including Linda’s father, Oliver Brown. On the lawyers’ advice, the parents tried to sign their children up at white schools. The schools turned them away because of their race.

In 1951 the families sued. They argued that assigning students by race breaks the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, even if the buildings look alike. The board argued that an 1896 case, Plessy v. Ferguson, still allowed separate schools. It also said Topeka’s schools were about equal.

The question

Can a state require separate public schools by race if it says the buildings are equal?

Important facts

  • This is an equal protection case. It is not mainly about safety.
  • The trial court found that segregation harmed Black children. But it still followed Plessy, because it found the schools about equal.
  • In 1950, in a case called Sweatt v. Painter, the Supreme Court had said that “equal” can mean more than buildings. A school’s reputation and the chances it offers count too.

Equal Protection Clause: The part of the Fourteenth Amendment that says no state may deny any person “the equal protection of the laws.”

What Happened?

It was 1950, in Topeka, Kansas. Black children and white children went to different elementary schools. A Kansas law allowed this. This was called “separate but equal.”

Linda Brown was a Black third grader. She walked through a railroad yard to catch a bus. The bus took her to a school for Black children. But a school for white children was about seven blocks from her home.

A group called the NAACP wanted to challenge the law. It asked 13 parents to help. One was Linda’s father, Oliver Brown. The parents tried to sign their children up at white schools. The schools said no because the children were Black.

So in 1951, the families went to court. They said the law broke the Fourteenth Amendment. The school board said an old case allowed separate schools. That case was called Plessy v. Ferguson. It was from 1896.

The question

Can a state keep children in separate schools by race if the buildings are equal?

What you need

“No State shall…deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.”

These words are from the Fourteenth Amendment. Equal protection means the law must treat all people the same.

Important facts

  • The trial court found that separate schools harmed Black children.
  • But that court also found Topeka’s schools about equal. So it followed Plessy.
  • The Supreme Court heard this case with three others. They came from South Carolina, Virginia, and Delaware.

Argument Answer
Government cannot classify and separate people based on their race. Evidence: The 14th Amendment (ratified July 9, 1868) states: "nor deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws."
States should have the right to organize their school systems based on local traditions. Evidence: In 1954, 17 states plus D.C. required school segregation by law, while 16 states prohibited it — showing states made their own choices.
Separating people by race automatically suggests that one race is inferior to another. Evidence: In Drs. Kenneth and Mamie Clark's doll tests, most Black children preferred the white dolls, and many said the Black dolls were "bad."
Education is too important to allow racial discrimination in public schools. Evidence: In the 1949–50 school year, a survey of six Deep South states found that spending per Black student was only 65 percent of spending per white student, showing "equal" schools were often not equal.
The Fourteenth Amendment was not originally intended to require integrated schools. Evidence: The same Congress that proposed the 14th Amendment in 1866 also funded segregated schools in Washington D.C. — showing they didn't intend to ban segregation.
Courts have accepted "separate but equal" for nearly 60 years, so judges should follow that precedent. Evidence: Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) established "separate but equal" as the law for 58 years. In 1951, the federal trial court in Topeka still followed it.
Research shows that segregation causes psychological harm to Black children. Evidence: The Topeka trial court found that segregation "has a detrimental effect upon the colored children." For the South Carolina case, Kenneth Clark repeated his doll test with Black children in Clarendon County.
In Topeka, a court found the Black and white schools had similar buildings, teachers, and materials. Evidence: The U.S. District Court in Kansas found Topeka's Black and white schools had substantially equal curricula, qualified teachers, and similar facilities.
Even when facilities look equal, segregated schools are inferior in reputation and opportunities. Evidence: In Sweatt v. Painter (1950), the Supreme Court ruled that a new Black law school lacked the "reputation," "influence of the alumni," and "prestige" of the white school, and it also had fewer faculty, courses, and books.
The Fourteenth Amendment requires that all people be treated equally under the law. Evidence: The Equal Protection Clause had already been used to strike down discrimination. In 1954, 21 states had laws requiring or permitting school segregation.
Forcing integration could cause social problems and conflicts in communities. Evidence: In November 1952, South Carolina voters approved a change to the state constitution that let the legislature close public schools rather than integrate them.
The "separate but equal" rule has been the law since 1896, and courts have followed it ever since. Evidence: Plessy v. Ferguson was precedent for 58 years. Courts follow a rule called "stare decisis" (Latin for "stand by what was decided") — meaning they try to follow past rulings to keep the law consistent.

Important concepts to understand when debating this case

Inherently Unequal

The idea that separation itself creates inequality. Under this idea, the quality of the buildings does not matter.

Psychological Harm

The emotional and mental damage that segregation can cause, especially to children.

Social Science Evidence

Research studies about how segregation affects children.

Separate Is Not Equal

Even if the buildings look the same, keeping children apart by race tells some of them they are lower.

Feeling of Inferiority

Inferiority means being treated as lower than others. The families argued that forced separation teaches Black children that the town sees them as lower. They said this is true even when the buildings look alike.

Separate is not equal

Connecting to Today

How these constitutional questions still matter

Questions about educational equality and segregation remain relevant more than 70 years after this case. Consider these modern situations:

School Funding

Resource Inequalities

In February 2019, a research group called EdBuild studied school money from the 2015-16 school year. Districts where more than 75 percent of students were white received about $23 billion more in state and local money than districts where more than 75 percent of students were not white. That came to about $2,226 less per student in the mostly nonwhite districts.

Source: Education Week, February 27, 2019

Think about: Does "equal protection" require equal school funding, or just the same legal access?

Demographics

Neighborhood Segregation

In 2022, the Government Accountability Office, which investigates for Congress, reported on the 2020-21 school year. More than a third of public school students, about 18.5 million, went to schools where at least 75 percent of students were one race or ethnicity. Separation that comes from where families live is called de facto segregation, meaning segregation in practice. Separation required by law is called de jure segregation.

Source: U.S. Government Accountability Office, GAO-22-104737, 2022

Think about: Should the Equal Protection Clause address segregation that happens without explicit laws?

Big Question

Choosing a School

Charter schools, vouchers, and magnet schools give some families a choice of school. Could choice bring students of different backgrounds together, or separate them more?

Think about: How do we balance family choice with the goal of integrated education?

Discussion Questions

  • If schools are no longer segregated by law but remain mostly one race, is that still a constitutional problem?
  • How might unequal school funding relate to the Equal Protection Clause?
  • Should the Constitution guarantee equal educational opportunities, or just equal legal treatment?

Questions about making schools fair for everyone are still important today. Here are some examples:

In the News

Some Schools Have More

In 2019, a research group looked at school money from 2015-16. Mostly white school districts got about $23 billion more than districts where most students were not white. That is about $2,226 less for each student in those districts.

Source: Education Week, February 27, 2019

What do you think: Is this fair? What should we do about it?

Where Kids Live

Neighborhoods Stay Separate

In 2022, a government report looked at the 2020-21 school year. More than a third of public school students went to schools where at least three out of four students were one race or ethnicity. Much of this comes from where families live.

Source: U.S. Government Accountability Office, GAO-22-104737, 2022

What do you think: Should we try to mix schools up more?

Big Question

Choosing Your School

Some families can choose their children’s school. Could choosing bring students together, or keep them more apart?

What do you think: Should families be able to pick any school they want?

Questions to Think About

  • If schools aren't separated by rules anymore but kids of different races still go to different schools, is that still unfair?
  • Is it okay for some schools to have way more money than others?
  • Should the government make sure every kid gets the same quality of education?
