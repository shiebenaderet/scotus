Morse v. Frederick (2007)

Reading:

Background Information

Understanding the facts and constitutional questions

🔗 Connecting to Our Guiding Question

When, if ever, should the government be allowed to limit a person's constitutional rights in order to protect the community?

This case asks whether a public school may punish a student for a banner that the principal believed encouraged illegal drug use. The student held it up across the street from school, during an event the school had approved. As you read, weigh the student’s right to free speech against the school’s job of teaching students to stay away from illegal drugs.

What Happened?

On January 24, 2002, the Olympic Torch Relay passed through Juneau, Alaska, on its way to the Winter Olympics in Salt Lake City, Utah. Runners carrying the torch were set to pass along the street in front of Juneau-Douglas High School during the school day. Principal Deborah Morse let students and staff leave class to watch. She called the relay an approved social event or class trip, and teachers watched over the students.

Joseph Frederick, an 18-year-old senior, never made it to school that morning because he got stuck in the snow in his driveway. He arrived in time to join a group of friends on the sidewalk across the street from the school. While the crowd waited, some students threw plastic soda bottles from a sponsor’s free samples and snowballs, and some got into fights. The evidence showed that Frederick’s group was not part of this, and it happened before the banner went up.

As the torchbearers and the television cameras passed, Frederick and his friends unrolled a 14-foot banner. It read: “BONG HiTS 4 JESUS.” Students on the school side of the street could read it easily. Morse crossed the street and told the group to take the banner down. Everyone but Frederick did. He asked her, “What about the Bill of Rights and freedom of speech?” Morse took the banner and suspended him for 10 days. She said the banner broke the school’s rule against messages that promote illegal drug use.

Frederick appealed to the superintendent, the head of the school district. The superintendent limited the suspension to the 8 days Frederick had already served, but he agreed with the principal, and so did the school board. Frederick then sued Morse and the school board in federal court. He said they had violated his First Amendment right to free speech.

The Big Question

Can a public school punish a student for a message at a school event because the school believes the message promotes illegal drug use, even when the student says it was a meaningless joke?

What You Need to Know

The First Amendment says:

“Congress shall make no law…abridging the freedom of speech…”

Public schools are run by state and local governments, and the Fourteenth Amendment makes the First Amendment apply to them too. The Juneau School Board also had a written rule, Policy 5520:

“The Board specifically prohibits any assembly or public expression that … advocates the use of substances that are illegal to minors … .”

Another board policy said students at approved social events and class trips follow the same conduct rules as during the regular school day. The two sides disagree about whether the relay counted as that kind of event.

Important Facts

  • Frederick had not been to school yet that day, and he stood on a public sidewalk across the street from the school.
  • The principal treated the relay as a school event. She pointed out that the pep band played, the cheerleaders came out in uniform, and teachers supervised.
  • Students said they did not need permission slips, as they did for field trips, and did not have to stay with their teachers.
  • Frederick said the words were “meaningless and funny” and that he wanted to get on television.
  • The principal said “bong hits” meant smoking marijuana and that the banner promoted drug use.
  • The superintendent wrote that Frederick’s speech “was not political.”
  • Frederick said the principal first gave him 5 days and doubled it after he quoted Thomas Jefferson. The principal said that was not why the suspension was 10 days.

Vocabulary

School-sanctioned event: An activity the school approves and supervises, even if it happens away from the building
Advocate: To speak in favor of something or urge others to do it
Suspension: Being kept out of school for a set number of days as a punishment
Precedent: An earlier court decision that judges look to when deciding a new case

What Happened?

It was January 24, 2002. The Olympic torch came to Juneau, Alaska. Runners carried it down the street in front of a high school. The principal, Deborah Morse, let students go outside to watch. She said it was a school event. Teachers watched the students.

Joseph Frederick was a senior. He was 18. He got stuck in the snow at home, so he never got inside the school. He stood with friends on the sidewalk across the street.

Then the TV cameras came. Frederick and his friends held up a long banner. It said “BONG HiTS 4 JESUS.” The principal crossed the street. She told them to take it down. Everyone did but Frederick.

So the principal took the banner. She suspended him. That means he could not come to school for some days. She said the banner was about illegal drugs. A school rule said students could not support illegal drugs.

Frederick went to court. He said the school broke his right to free speech.

The question

Can a school punish a student for a message at a school event? The school said the message was about illegal drugs.

What you need

“Congress shall make no law…abridging the freedom of speech…”

These words are from the First Amendment. Abridging means cutting back. So the government may not cut back free speech. Public schools are part of the government. So this rule covers them too.

The school also had its own rule. It said students may not advocate drugs that are illegal for kids. To advocate means to speak in favor of something.

Important facts

  • Frederick stood across the street. He was not on school land.
  • The principal said the band played and teachers watched the students.
  • Frederick said the words were a joke. He said he wanted to be on TV.
  • The principal said the words were about drugs.

Advocate: To speak in favor of something.
Suspension: Being kept out of school as a punishment.

Argument Answer
The relay was an approved school event during school hours, and teachers supervised the students. Evidence: Principal Morse called the relay an approved social event or class trip. She pointed out that the pep band played and the cheerleaders came out in uniform.
Frederick stood on a public sidewalk across the street and had not been inside the school that day. Evidence: Frederick’s car was stuck in the snow, so he never went into the school before the relay. Nobody disputed that the banner was off school property.
The school board had a written rule against student messages that advocate using substances illegal for minors. Evidence: Juneau School Board Policy 5520 bans “any assembly or public expression that … advocates the use of substances that are illegal to minors.”
Frederick said the words were meaningless and funny, not a message about drugs. Evidence: Frederick said the banner was “meaningless and funny” and that he wanted to get on television.
Schools teach students that illegal drugs are dangerous, and a message that promotes drugs works against that lesson. Evidence: The school district said the banner was “inconsistent with the district’s basic educational mission to promote a healthy, drug-free life style.”
The school never claimed the banner disrupted classwork. Evidence: When asked how the banner disrupted school, the school district pointed to its drug-prevention mission, not to any class that was interrupted.
Frederick refused to take the banner down when the principal told him to. Evidence: Everyone else in Frederick’s group lowered the banner when Morse asked. Frederick did not.
A school should not be able to punish a message just because officials dislike it. Evidence: Frederick argued that under Tinker v. Des Moines (1969), a school may limit student speech only when it would substantially disrupt school.
Students do not have the same free speech rights at school as adults have in public. Evidence: In Bethel School District v. Fraser (1986), the Supreme Court allowed a school to punish a student for a lewd speech at a school assembly.
The superintendent said the banner was not political speech. Evidence: The superintendent wrote that Frederick’s speech “was not political” and did not argue for changing any law.
Students did not need permission slips and did not have to stay with their teachers during the relay. Evidence: Students said they were simply let out of school to watch, and that some who got bored left.
The banner was held up where the students on the school side of the street could easily read it. Evidence: The banner was 14 feet long, and students on the school side of the street could easily read it.
Punishing a student for a joke could make students afraid to say anything a principal might misread. Evidence: Frederick asked the principal, “What about the Bill of Rights and freedom of speech?”

Key Terms for Arguments

Important concepts to understand when debating this case

School Event

The school says the relay counted as school because the principal approved it, teachers supervised it, and school rules applied. Frederick says he was on a public sidewalk, had not been to school that day, and was watching a public parade.

Substantial Disruption

The test Frederick wants the Court to use, and the one the court of appeals used. A school may punish student speech only if it would seriously interfere with school. The school says that test should not decide a case about promoting illegal drugs.

Educational Mission

The school’s argument that it teaches students to avoid illegal drugs, so it may stop student messages that promote them at school events.

School event

The school says the relay was school because the principal approved it and teachers watched. Frederick says he was on a public sidewalk at a public parade.

Substantial disruption

Frederick’s test. A school may punish speech only if it would seriously get in the way of school.

Educational mission

The school’s argument. It teaches students to stay away from drugs. So it may stop messages that support drugs.

School event

Something the school plans and watches over. The two sides do not agree that the relay was one.

Substantial disruption

A big problem that stops school. Frederick says the school needs this before it punishes speech.

Educational mission

What a school is trying to teach. The school says it teaches kids to stay away from drugs.

Connecting to Today

How these constitutional questions still matter

The questions in this case still come up in schools. Consider these situations:

Changing Laws

Alaska Votes on Marijuana

In November 2014, Alaska voters approved Ballot Measure 2, which made marijuana legal for adults. The measure kept it illegal to sell marijuana to anyone under 21.

Source: Anchorage Daily News, November 5, 2014

Think about: If a drug is legal for adults but not for students, should a school treat a message about it the same way?

Big Question

Off Campus, but Still School?

Students go on field trips, travel to games, and march in parades. Some of these events happen far from the school building.

Think about: When a student is away from school but at a school event, should school rules still apply?

Big Question

Political or Not?

Some student messages are about politics, and some are jokes or nonsense. Should those be treated differently?

Think about: Should a school have more power over a joke than over a political message?

Discussion Questions

  • Should it matter that Frederick stood on a public sidewalk instead of school property?
  • Should a school be able to stop messages that promote illegal drugs even if nothing is disrupted?
  • How should a court decide what a confusing message really means?
