Payton v. New York (1980)

Reading:

Background Information

Understanding the facts and constitutional questions

Connecting to Our Guiding Question

When, if ever, should the government be allowed to limit a person's constitutional rights in order to protect the community?

This case asks: Should police be able to limit Fourth Amendment privacy rights (by entering homes without warrants) to protect the community from criminals? Consider how this case balances individual privacy rights against community safety needs.

This case asks: May police go into a home to make a planned arrest without a warrant if they already have probable cause? A warrant is written permission from a judge. Police say waiting can let dangerous suspects escape. But the Fourth Amendment gives the home special protection. When is community safety enough reason to skip the judge?

This case asks: Can police go into a home to arrest someone without a warrant? A warrant is permission from a judge. The police had good reasons to think a man had killed someone. But they did not ask a judge first. So when is keeping people safe a good enough reason to skip the judge?

What Happened?

In 1970, the police in New York City suspected Theodore Payton of murder. After two days of investigation, detectives had gathered enough evidence to show probable cause that he had murdered the manager of a gas station. However, instead of going to a judge to get an arrest warrant, police officers went directly to Payton's home.

On January 15, 1970, six officers went to Payton's Bronx apartment to arrest him. No one answered, so they used crowbars to break open the door. Payton was not home. A .30-caliber shell casing was in plain view, and it was later used at his trial. Officers also searched drawers and a closet, but the prosecutor agreed that evidence could not be used. Payton later turned himself in, was charged with murder, and was convicted.

The Big Question

Can police enter a suspect's home to make an arrest without a warrant? Does the Fourth Amendment require warrants for home arrests?

What You Need to Know

The Fourth Amendment says:

"The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated, and no Warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause..."

This means the government cannot search your home or take your things without a good reason. Usually, they need a warrant from a judge.

There's an old saying: "A man's home is his castle." This means people should feel safe from government intrusion in their own homes.

Important Facts

  • Six officers went to Payton's apartment at 7:30 AM
  • They had no arrest warrant
  • They broke down the door with a crowbar
  • Payton wasn't home
  • They found a shell casing in plain view
  • New York law allowed warrantless home arrests with probable cause
  • A second case (Obie Riddick, also from New York) was combined with Payton's — police entered Riddick's Queens home without a warrant when his young son opened the door
  • The Supreme Court combined both cases because they raised the same question about warrantless home arrests

Vocabulary

Warrant: A document signed by a judge that gives police legal permission to arrest someone or search a place
Probable Cause: Facts that would make a reasonable person believe a crime was committed, or that evidence of it is in a certain place. It is a higher bar than reasonable suspicion.
Felony: A serious crime that can result in prison time
Exigent Circumstances: Emergency situations where waiting for a warrant would be dangerous

What Happened?

In January 1970, New York City police believed Theodore Payton had murdered the manager of a gas station. They had probable cause, which means enough facts to believe he did it. But they did not get an arrest warrant from a judge.

On January 15, 1970, at about 7:30 a.m., six officers went to his apartment in the Bronx. No one answered the door, so they broke it open with crowbars. Payton was not home. A .30-caliber shell casing was sitting in plain view. It was later used at his trial. Officers also searched drawers and a closet, but the prosecutor agreed those items could not be used. Payton later turned himself in.

At the time, a New York law let police enter a home without a warrant to arrest someone for a felony if they had probable cause. A felony is a serious crime. Payton argued that the Fourth Amendment gives the home extra protection. New York argued that waiting for a warrant lets dangerous suspects escape or destroy evidence.

A second case was decided together with Payton’s. In Queens, police entered Obie Riddick’s home without a warrant after his young son opened the door.

The question

May police enter a home to make a planned felony arrest without a warrant, if they have probable cause?

Important facts

  • This was a planned arrest. It was not a chase or an emergency.
  • Police had probable cause, but no warrant.
  • Payton’s side says the home deserves the Fourth Amendment’s strongest protection.
  • The evidence used at trial was a shell casing that police saw in plain view after they entered.

What Happened?

It was January 1970 in New York City. A man who ran a gas station was killed. The police believed Theodore Payton did it. They had probable cause. That means they had enough facts to believe it.

But the police did not get a warrant. A warrant is permission from a judge.

On January 15, 1970, six officers went to Payton’s apartment. It was early in the morning. No one opened the door. So the police broke it open. Payton was not home. But the police saw a bullet shell sitting out in the open. It was used later at his trial. Payton turned himself in later.

Argument Answer
The Fourth Amendment protects people's homes from unreasonable government searches. Evidence: The Fourth Amendment, ratified in 1791, specifically lists "houses" as protected from unreasonable searches. The Founders wrote this because British customs officers had used general warrants called writs of assistance to search colonists' homes and shops before the Revolution.
Police had probable cause to believe that Payton committed murder. Evidence: Police had "probable cause" (a reasonable belief based on facts) — after two days of intensive investigation, detectives had assembled evidence that Payton had murdered the manager of a gas station.
The Supreme Court had long required warrants for most searches of homes. Evidence: In Mapp v. Ohio (1961), the Court ruled that evidence found during illegal searches cannot be used in court. In Katz v. United States (1967), the Court expanded Fourth Amendment protections — showing a pattern of protecting privacy.
Allowing warrantless arrests in homes would effectively eliminate the warrant requirement. Evidence: If police can enter homes without warrants for arrests, they could easily claim to be "arresting" someone just to search. In Payton's case, officers also searched drawers and a closet during their warrantless entry; the prosecutor admitted that search was illegal, and only the shell casing in plain view was used at trial.
Police have traditionally had the power to arrest suspects without warrants in public places. Evidence: In United States v. Watson (1976), the Supreme Court ruled that police don't need warrants to arrest someone in a public place if they have probable cause. New York argued this same rule should apply inside homes.
The suspect could destroy evidence or flee if police had to wait for a warrant. Evidence: Payton was suspected of murder — a crime that could carry a life sentence. New York argued that waiting for a warrant can let suspects flee or destroy evidence. The law calls these situations "exigent circumstances" (emergencies that justify quick action).
There is an important difference between searching a home and entering to arrest someone. Evidence: New York argued that arrest warrants and search warrants are different. An arrest warrant authorizes taking a person; a search warrant authorizes looking through belongings. The Constitution's text mentions "searches and seizures" separately.
Fewer and fewer states allowed police to enter homes without warrants to make arrests. Evidence: At the time of this case, 24 states (including New York) allowed warrantless home entries to arrest, 15 states clearly prohibited them, and 11 had taken no position. But the number allowing them had been dropping.
Police can enter homes without warrants in true emergencies. Evidence: Courts recognize "exigent circumstances" — emergencies like someone's life being in danger, a suspect actively fleeing, or evidence being destroyed right now. Even people who want warrant requirements agree that true emergencies are an exception.
Throughout American history, police have made home arrests without warrants. Evidence: New York's law allowing warrantless home arrests for felonies had existed since 1881, when New York adopted its Code of Criminal Procedure. The state argued this long history showed the practice was accepted and constitutional — if it was wrong, courts would have stopped it long ago.
Getting a warrant is not difficult for police. Evidence: New York's highest court treated both Payton's and Riddick's arrests as routine arrests in which there was ample time to obtain a warrant. Police had their evidence a day before they went to Payton's door.
A person's home deserves the strongest Fourth Amendment protection. Evidence: The saying that "every man's house is his castle" comes from English cases of the early 1600s. In 1972, the Supreme Court said that "physical entry of the home is the chief evil against which the wording of the Fourth Amendment is directed."

Key Terms for Arguments

Important concepts to understand when debating this case

Exigent Circumstances

Emergency situations where waiting for a warrant would be dangerous. Examples include: someone's life is in danger, evidence is being destroyed, or a suspect is fleeing.

Exigent circumstances (an emergency)

A time when waiting for a warrant would be dangerous. For example, someone could get hurt, evidence could be destroyed, or a suspect could escape.

Exigent circumstances (an emergency)

A time when police cannot wait. Someone could get hurt. Or a suspect could run away.

Connecting to Today

How these constitutional questions still matter

The Fourth Amendment questions in this case remain highly relevant today. Consider these modern situations:

Police Policy

No-Knock Raids

In March 2020, Louisville police serving a no-knock warrant shot and killed Breonna Taylor in her apartment. On June 11, 2020, the Louisville Metro Council voted unanimously to ban no-knock warrants, a law known as Breonna's Law.

Think about: How does the "knock and announce" rule relate to the Fourth Amendment's protection of homes?

Technology

Smart Home Devices

In January 2024, Ring said it would stop letting police ask users for doorbell video through its Neighbors app. In September 2025, Ring began rolling out a new feature, Community Requests, that again lets police ask users for video. Should police need warrants to access data from devices like these?

Think about: Does the Fourth Amendment's protection of "houses" extend to digital information inside homes?

Current Debate

Emergency Exceptions

In 2021, in Caniglia v. Strom, the Supreme Court ruled unanimously that a police "community caretaking" duty, such as a wellness check, does not by itself allow officers to enter a home without a warrant.

Think about: Who should decide what counts as an "emergency" - police officers in the moment, or judges beforehand?

Discussion Questions

  • Should the rules for entering homes be different for serious crimes like murder versus less serious offenses?
  • How do you balance giving police the tools they need to catch criminals with protecting everyone's privacy at home?
  • If police have strong evidence someone committed a crime, why should they have to wait for a judge's approval?

The questions in this case still come up. Here are three real examples.

In the News

Police Entering Homes

In March 2020, police in Louisville, Kentucky, were serving a no-knock warrant. That kind of warrant lets police go in without knocking first. They shot and killed Breonna Taylor in her apartment. On June 11, 2020, the Louisville city council voted to ban no-knock warrants.

Source: NPR, June 11, 2020

What do you think: Should police always have to knock and wait?

New Technology

Cameras and Smart Devices

In January 2024, the company Ring stopped letting police ask users for doorbell videos through its Neighbors app. In September 2025, Ring started a new feature that lets police ask again. Users can say no. Should police need a warrant to get videos like these?

Source: WBUR, September 30, 2025

What do you think: Is information from your home devices private like your home itself?

Big Question

When Is It an Emergency?

In 2021, in a case called Caniglia v. Strom, the Supreme Court said that checking on someone’s safety is not, by itself, enough reason for police to enter a home without a warrant. Who should decide what counts as an emergency?

Source: Oyez, Caniglia v. Strom (2021)

What do you think: Should police decide, or should they ask a judge first?
