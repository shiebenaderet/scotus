Mahanoy v. B.L. (2021)

Background Information

Understanding the facts and constitutional questions

🔗 Connecting to Our Guiding Question

When, if ever, should the government be allowed to limit a person's constitutional rights in order to protect the community?

This case asks whether a public school may punish a student's off-campus social media posts to protect the school community from disruption and keep team discipline. As you read, weigh a student's right to express frustration outside school against the school's interest in order and a positive team culture.

This case asks: May a school punish a student for a vulgar Snapchat posted off campus on a weekend? The school said the posts hurt the cheer team. B.L. says Saturday speech on a personal phone is her family’s business. When may a school reach off campus to protect order at school?

This case asks: Can a school punish a student for a post she made on a weekend, away from school? B.L. posted swear words about school and cheer on Snapchat. The school said the posts hurt the cheer team. B.L. said a post made on a Saturday, on her own phone, was her family’s business. So when can a school reach past its own walls to keep order?

What Happened?

In 2017, a 14-year-old student named B.L. was a cheerleader at Mahanoy Area High School in Pennsylvania. She tried out for the varsity cheerleading team but did not make it. She would have to remain on the junior varsity team for another year.

That weekend, still upset about not making varsity, B.L. posted two images to her Snapchat story (visible to about 250 friends for 24 hours). Many of those friends attended her school, and some were on the cheerleading team.

One student took pictures of the posts and shared them with other cheerleaders, and another student showed them to her mother, a cheerleading coach. Several cheerleaders and other students came to the coaches "visibly upset." The coaches decided that because the posts used profanity in connection with a school activity, they broke team and school rules.

The school suspended B.L. from the junior varsity cheerleading team for the entire upcoming year. The school said her posts violated team and school rules about conduct.

B.L. and her parents sued the school district, arguing that her First Amendment rights to free speech had been violated. They claimed the school had no authority to punish her for speech that occurred off-campus and outside of school hours.

The Big Question

Can schools punish students for speech posted on social media when they are off-campus and outside of school hours?

What You Need to Know

The First Amendment says:

"Congress shall make no law...abridging the freedom of speech..."

This protects people's right to express themselves, even when that expression is offensive or critical.

The key question is whether schools have authority over student speech that happens away from school, or whether their authority is limited to on-campus activities.

Important Facts

  • B.L. made the posts on a weekend, away from school property
  • She used vulgar language and made obscene gestures
  • The posts were seen by many students and some cheerleaders
  • At school, questions about the posts came up in an Algebra class for, at most, 5 to 10 minutes over a couple of days
  • B.L. was suspended from the team for an entire year
  • This was the first time the Supreme Court decided whether a public school could punish a student's off-campus social media post

Vocabulary

Off-Campus Speech: Expression that occurs away from school property and outside school hours
Social Media: Online platforms like Snapchat, Instagram, and TikTok where people share content
Substantial Disruption: Significant interference with school activities (the test from Tinker v. Des Moines)
Extracurricular Activities: School activities like sports and clubs that are optional

What Happened?

B.L., a 14-year-old freshman, did not make varsity cheer. That Saturday, off campus at a convenience store, she posted two Snaps: one with raised middle fingers and “F*** school f*** softball f*** cheer f*** everything,” and a second that mentioned another student who also missed varsity.

One student took pictures of the posts and shared them. Another student showed them to her mother, a cheer coach. B.L. was suspended from JV cheer for a year. The school said Tinker’s disruption idea and team rules reach weekend social media. B.L. said off-campus vulgar venting is private speech the school may not punish.

The question

May a school punish a student for a vulgar weekend Snapchat about cheer and school?

Important facts

  • The posts were off campus, on a weekend, on Snapchat.
  • They used profanity and targeted school and cheer — not a political protest like Tinker’s armbands.
  • The punishment was a year off the team, not a classroom suspension.

Vocabulary

Off-campus speech: Expression made away from school, often on a phone. The fight is whether Tinker still applies.
Substantial disruption: Tinker’s on-campus test. The school wants it to follow students home; B.L. says it should not.

What Happened?

It was 2017. B.L. was 14. She was a cheerleader at Mahanoy Area High School in Pennsylvania. She tried out for the varsity team, but she did not make it. Varsity is the top team. So she would stay on junior varsity, or JV, for another year.

That weekend, B.L. was at a store, away from school. She posted two pictures on Snapchat. One showed her middle fingers. Its words used a swear word about school, softball, cheer and everything. The other post complained that she had to spend another year on JV. About 250 friends could see the posts.

One student took pictures of the posts with another phone. That student shared them with other cheerleaders. Another student showed them to her mother. Her mother was a cheer coach. Some students came to the coaches upset.

The coaches said the posts broke team and school rules. So the school took B.L. off the JV team for a whole year. Then B.L. and her parents went to court. They said the First Amendment protects what she posted away from school.

The question

Can a school punish a student for a post she made away from school, on a weekend?

What you need

“Congress shall make no law…abridging the freedom of speech…”

These words are from the First Amendment. Abridging means cutting back. So the government may not cut back free speech. A public school is part of the government. The question is how far a school’s power reaches outside school.

Important facts

  • She posted on a weekend, away from school.
  • The posts used swear words and a rude hand sign.
  • The posts did not name the school.
  • At school, students asked about the posts in one math class. It took 5 to 10 minutes over a couple of days.
  • She was taken off the team for a year.

Vocabulary

Off-campus speech: Speech away from school, like a post made at home.
Substantial disruption: A big problem that stops school work. This rule comes from the Tinker case.

B.L.

Schools cannot punish students for speech made off school grounds on their own time. The First Amendment protects her right to vent frustration on social media outside of school hours.

The Two Sides

Mahanoy School District

Tinker’s disruption rule should follow students onto their phones when a post is about the school and the team. Cheer rules can cover weekend posts that hurt the team.

B.L.

A post made off campus, on her own time, is private speech. Swearing about cheer is not a substantial disruption. A year off the team punishes her for a message. It does not punish a walkout or a fight.

The Two Sides

Mahanoy School District

The posts were about school and the team. So the school should be able to act, even though she posted away from school. Team rules can cover what players do on weekends.

B.L.

She posted on a weekend, away from school, on her own phone. Swear words did not stop school. A year off the team is too much.

Argument Answer
Other courts have allowed schools to punish students for criticizing school officials online. Evidence: In Kowalski v. Berkeley County Schools (2011), a court ruled schools could punish a student for creating a MySpace page bullying a classmate. In Doninger v. Niehoff (2011), a student was punished for a blog post calling administrators "douchebags."
Social media posts can be seen by many students and can spread quickly to cause disruption. Evidence: B.L. posted to her Snapchat story, which about 250 friends could see for 24 hours — many of whom attended her school. A student took pictures of the posts with a separate phone, and the pictures spread to the coaches. Even "disappearing" posts can be saved and shared.
B.L. never required anyone to see her posts - they were shared with her chosen friends. Evidence: Snapchat posts were only visible to B.L.'s chosen friends — not the public. They were designed to disappear in 24 hours. A classmate photographed them with a separate phone and shared them with other cheerleaders; B.L. didn't force anyone to see them.
The Court's decision in Tinker bars schools from punishing speech based on disagreement with the message. Evidence: In Tinker v. Des Moines (1969), the Supreme Court ruled students don't "shed their constitutional rights...at the schoolhouse gate." Schools can only punish speech that causes "substantial disruption" — not just speech they find offensive or rude.
With online learning, there is no clear separation between on-campus and off-campus activities. Evidence: During the COVID-19 pandemic (2020-2021), millions of students learned from home. The line between "school" and "home" has blurred.
Allowing schools to punish off-campus speech gives them too much control over students' lives. Evidence: In 2020, the federal appeals court that heard B.L.'s case ruled that Tinker's disruption test does not apply to speech made off campus. That would leave a student's weekend speech outside school control.
The First Amendment protects students' right to express frustration and criticism. Evidence: The Supreme Court has ruled that even vulgar speech is protected by the First Amendment. In Cohen v. California (1971), the Court protected a man wearing a jacket saying "F*** the Draft" — showing that offensive words don't lose constitutional protection.
Several federal appeals courts had ruled that schools can regulate student speech that affects the school, even when made off-campus. Evidence: Before Mahanoy, multiple federal appeals courts ruled that schools could punish off-campus speech if it was "reasonably foreseeable" that it would reach school and cause disruption. This gave schools significant power over online speech.
Schools need authority to maintain discipline and positive culture in their programs. Evidence: In Vernonia School District v. Acton (1995), the Court ruled that students in extracurricular activities can be held to higher standards. B.L. had acknowledged team rules requiring cheerleaders to "have respect for [their] school, coaches, . . . [and] other cheerleaders" and to avoid "foul language and inappropriate gestures."
B.L. made her posts on a weekend, away from school property, using her personal social media account. Evidence: B.L. posted on a Saturday afternoon from a local convenience store — not school property. She used her personal phone and personal Snapchat account — not any school technology. The school had zero involvement in where, when, or how she posted.
Snapchat posts disappear quickly and are not permanent like other social media. Evidence: Snapchat messages are designed to disappear after 24 hours (or even seconds). But a classmate photographed B.L.'s posts with a separate phone, and the pictures spread to the coaches.
Cheerleaders represent the school and should maintain appropriate conduct at all times. Evidence: Before joining, B.L. acknowledged team rules that said, "Remember, you are representing your school when at games, fundraisers, and other events." Cheerleaders wear school uniforms and perform at school events — they are public representatives of the school's image.
B.L.'s posts contained no threats, harassment, or even mention of her specific school. Evidence: B.L. wrote "f*** school f*** softball f*** cheer f*** everything." The posts used profanity about school and teams. They did not threaten violence or attack a classmate. A second snap mentioned another student who also did not make varsity, but it was a complaint about tryouts, not harassment.

Connecting to Today

How these constitutional questions still matter

Here are three situations that raise the same question about a school’s power outside school:

Big Question

Posting From Home

Imagine a student who writes a post at night, from home, complaining about a teacher, a coach or a school rule. Other students see it the next morning.

Think about: Where does school authority end and personal freedom begin in the digital age?

Online Behavior

Cyberbullying

In a 2023 national survey by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 16% of high school students said they had been bullied electronically, through texting or social media, during the past year. Bullying that starts at home can follow students into school. That is why some schools say they must be able to respond to it.

Source: CDC, Youth Risk Behavior Survey Data Summary & Trends Report, 2013–2023

Think about: Should schools be able to punish cyberbullying that happens entirely outside school?

Washington State

Watching Student Accounts

In March 2025, The Seattle Times and the Associated Press reported on Vancouver Public Schools in southwest Washington. The district used a program called Gaggle to scan what students searched and wrote on school-issued laptops and school accounts, 24 hours a day. Between October 2023 and October 2024, nearly 2,200 students, about 10% of the district, were the subject of a Gaggle alert. A principal said the district could not “put a price on protecting students.” Reporters who asked for records were also given access to almost 3,500 sensitive student documents that were not protected by passwords.

Source: The Seattle Times and the Associated Press, March 12, 2025

Think about: Is there a difference between punishing speech and monitoring for safety?

Discussion Questions

  • If a student posts something at 10pm from their bedroom, should the school be able to respond?
  • How do you balance students' free speech rights with protecting other students from online harassment?
  • Does it matter if the post mentions the school by name versus being a general complaint?
