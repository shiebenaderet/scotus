Jacobson v. Massachusetts (1905)

Reading:

Background Information

Understanding the facts and constitutional questions

🔗 Connecting to Our Guiding Question

When, if ever, should the government be allowed to limit a person's constitutional rights in order to protect the community?

This case asks whether a state may fine an adult who refuses a smallpox vaccination during an outbreak. As you read, weigh one person’s liberty against the safety of the community. Jacobson wanted to decide what was done to his own body. The city wanted to stop a deadly disease from spreading.

This case asks a question. May a state fine an adult who will not be vaccinated during a smallpox outbreak? Jacobson says the Constitution protects his liberty. Massachusetts says it must protect everyone from a deadly disease.

This case asks: Can a state fine a man who says no to a vaccine? Smallpox was spreading in his city. He said the Constitution protects his right to say no. The state said it had to keep everyone safe.

What Happened?

In 1901, smallpox broke out in Boston and the cities around it. Smallpox is a contagious disease that causes a high fever and a blistering rash. It killed many of the people who caught it. The outbreak lasted until 1903, and during that time 270 people died of smallpox in Boston alone.

Massachusetts already had a law about vaccination. Vaccination protects a person from a disease by training the body to fight it. The law let a city’s board of health require everyone to be vaccinated if the board decided it was necessary for public health or safety. The city had to provide the vaccinations for free. An adult over 21 who refused had to pay a $5 fine. Children whose doctor certified that they were “unfit subjects for vaccination” were excused. The law had no matching exception for adults.

On February 27, 1902, the Cambridge Board of Health used that law. Its order said smallpox had been “prevalent to some extent” in the city and “still continues to increase.” It required everyone who had not been successfully vaccinated since March 1, 1897, to be vaccinated or vaccinated again.

Henning Jacobson was the pastor of a Swedish Lutheran church in Cambridge. The chairman of the board of health told him that he would be prosecuted if he refused. The chairman also offered to vaccinate Jacobson for free. Jacobson declined.

Jacobson was charged in a local court in 1902 and pleaded not guilty. He asked to prove to the jury that vaccination “quite often” caused serious harm. He also said that a vaccination he received as a child had caused him “great and extreme suffering.” He said he had seen the same thing happen to his son. The judge ruled that none of this evidence could be heard. The jury found him guilty. Massachusetts’s highest court upheld the trial in 1903, and Jacobson was sentenced to pay the $5 fine. He took his case to the Supreme Court of the United States. The Court heard arguments on December 6, 1904.

The Big Question

May a state make adults choose between vaccination and a fine during a disease outbreak, or does that take away the liberty the Fourteenth Amendment protects?

What You Need to Know

The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, says:

“…nor shall any State deprive any person of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law…”

Jacobson argued that the vaccination law took away his liberty and that it treated adults unfairly, because children could be excused with a doctor’s note and adults could not. Massachusetts argued that protecting the public from deadly disease is part of its police power. Police power is the power every state has to make laws for the health and safety of its people.

Important Facts

  • Smallpox was spreading in Cambridge when the board made its order.
  • Vaccination was free, and the penalty for refusing was a $5 fine.
  • Children with a doctor’s certificate could be excused, but adults had no medical exception in the law.
  • The trial judge did not let Jacobson show the jury his evidence about harm from vaccination.
  • In 1901, nine children in Camden, New Jersey, died of tetanus after receiving contaminated smallpox vaccine. In 1902, Congress passed its first law regulating how vaccines were made.

Vocabulary

Police Power: A state’s power to make laws that protect the health, safety and welfare of the people who live there
Due Process: The Constitution’s promise that the government must follow fair rules before it takes away a person’s life, liberty or property
Vaccination: Giving a person a vaccine so the body learns to fight a disease
Epidemic: An outbreak of a disease that spreads to many people in one area at the same time

What Happened?

It was 1901. A disease called smallpox was spreading in Boston. Smallpox spreads from person to person. It causes a high fever and blisters. Many people who caught it died. In Boston, 270 people died of it from 1901 to 1903.

There was a way to fight it. It was a vaccine. A vaccine helps the body fight a disease. Getting a vaccine is called vaccination.

Massachusetts had a law. It said a city could tell everyone to get the vaccine. The vaccine had to be free. A grown-up who said no had to pay $5. A child with a doctor’s note could skip it. A grown-up could not.

In February 1902, the city of Cambridge used the law. Smallpox was spreading there. So the city told people to get the vaccine.

Henning Jacobson was a pastor in Cambridge. A pastor leads a church. A city health leader offered him the vaccine for free. Jacobson said no.

So Jacobson was taken to court. He said a vaccine had hurt him badly when he was a child. He said it hurt his son too. But the judge would not let the jury hear about this. The jury said he was guilty. He had to pay $5.

Jacobson did not give up. He took his case to the Supreme Court. It heard both sides on December 6, 1904.

Argument Answer
A free country lets each adult decide what is done to his own body. Evidence: In Allgeyer v. Louisiana (1897), the Supreme Court said the “liberty” in the Fourteenth Amendment means more than “the right of the citizen to be free from the mere physical restraint of his person, as by incarceration.”
Smallpox was spreading in Cambridge when the vaccination order was made. Evidence: The Cambridge Board of Health’s order of February 27, 1902, said smallpox had been “prevalent to some extent” in the city and “still continues to increase.”
The law excused children with a doctor’s note but gave adults no medical exception. Evidence: The statute excused “children who present a certificate, signed by a registered physician, that they are unfit subjects for vaccination.” It said nothing about adults with the same problem.
The state offered vaccination for free, so cost kept no one from obeying. Evidence: The law said the board of health “shall provide them with the means of free vaccination.”
Vaccines in that era were not always safe or pure. Evidence: In 1901, nine children in Camden, New Jersey, died of tetanus after receiving contaminated smallpox vaccine (U.S. Food and Drug Administration history).
Health laws have long been part of what states control. Evidence: In Gibbons v. Ogden (1824), Chief Justice John Marshall listed “quarantine laws, health laws of every description” among the laws that belong to the states.
Jacobson should have been allowed to show the jury his evidence about harm. Evidence: The trial judge ruled that every fact Jacobson offered to prove was “immaterial” and excluded all of it, according to the case record.
If anyone could refuse, the order could not stop the outbreak. Evidence: The board’s order said it was “necessary for the speedy extermination of the disease that all persons not protected by vaccination should be vaccinated.”
A vaccination he received as a child caused him serious harm. Evidence: Jacobson offered to prove that a childhood vaccination caused him “great and extreme suffering for a long period,” and that he had seen the same result in his son.
Courts in other states had already upheld vaccination rules. Evidence: In Viemeister v. White (1904), New York’s highest court upheld a rule that kept unvaccinated children out of public school.
Elected lawmakers and health boards, not juries, should weigh medical evidence for a whole city. Evidence: The law gave the power to the board of health “if, in its opinion, it is necessary for the public health or safety.”
Punishing a person for refusing a medical procedure goes too far, even if the penalty is small. Evidence: Jacobson was found guilty of a crime, sentenced to pay $5, and ordered held until the fine was paid, according to the case record.

Connecting to Today

How these constitutional questions still matter

Public health laws are still debated across the United States. Here are two dated examples:

Washington State

School Vaccine Rules

On July 28, 2019, a new Washington law took effect that ended personal and philosophical exemptions from the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine for students in public and private schools and child care. Medical and religious exemptions stayed in place.

Think about: Should a school vaccine rule allow exemptions for personal beliefs, religion or medical reasons? Which ones, and why?

Source: Washington State Legislature, Final Bill Report, EHB 1638 (2019)

COVID-19

Workplace Vaccine Rules

On January 13, 2022, the Supreme Court blocked a federal rule that would have required workers at large companies to be vaccinated against COVID-19 or tested weekly. The same day, it allowed a different rule requiring vaccination for most workers at health care facilities paid by Medicare and Medicaid. Both decisions turned on what federal agencies are allowed to do under the laws Congress passed.

Think about: Should the rules be different for a hospital worker and an office worker? Why?

Source: NPR, January 13, 2022

Big Question

Who Decides?

When a disease spreads, who should decide what each person must do: the person, doctors, elected lawmakers, or courts?

Think about: Does your answer change if the disease is more deadly?
